## Method for the new axis

**Actual-use-after-30-days** asks: on Oct 1, would either builder (captain, or the partner - a voice-AI QA engineer at xAI) plausibly still open this tool, or would a stranger plausibly `git clone` it, a month after the hackathon ends? Rating scale, applied consistently:

- **High** - solves a problem one of the builders has *this week*, in their actual job/side-projects, with low ongoing maintenance burden; or the tool-shape has a track record of real open-source or commercial staying power.
- **Medium** - useful, but needs either ongoing data/infra upkeep (a bad sign for two people with day jobs) or only partially matches a recurring problem either builder actually has.
- **Low** - the classic hackathon pattern: cool once, then the tab closes. Usually because the problem is a one-off ("write my rehearsal report"), the value decays without continuous new data, or a superior commercial/OSS alternative already fully absorbs the use case so there's no reason to keep a hand-rolled version alive.

Evidence gathered by real search (not vibes): GitHub star/commit-activity signal for close open-source analogs, and whether the surrounding commercial category is thriving (repeat paying customers, active funding, shipped platform features) or dormant. A thriving *commercial* category is actually a mixed signal for *this team's* persistent use - it proves the problem is real and recurring, but it also means the fully-baked version already exists elsewhere, so the builders' own copy is more likely to get replaced by the commercial tool than kept alive by hand.

## The 21st candidate: voice-agent compliance & legal-risk tooling

Captain's added angle: look at utilitarian problems *buyers* of voice agents have (not the model vendors), specifically compliance/legal risk - consent law, PII leakage, AI-disclosure requirements, TCPA/HIPAA-style regs.

**Real research findings (Sep 2026):**

- **TCPA exposure is large and live.** Every outbound AI voice call needs prior express consent; the FCC's Feb 2024 ruling makes AI-voice calls legally "robocalls" under TCPA; penalties run $500-$1,500 per call with no aggregate cap; a Feb 2026 Fifth Circuit ruling narrowed the written-consent requirement in three states only, creating a real, current patchwork companies have to track. Multiple law firms and voice-AI vendors (Retell, Henson Legal, Ringlyn, LeadLock) are actively publishing 2026 compliance guides - a strong signal that companies deploying voice agents are actively worried about this right now, not hypothetically.
- **State AI-disclosure rules are fragmenting in exactly the way that creates demand for a checking tool.** California requires AI disclosure within the first 15 seconds of a call; Florida (FTSA) and Oklahoma (OTSA) require written consent for automated marketing contact; Colorado's AI Act (effective 2026) may classify voice AI as "high-risk." A company running one voice agent across all 50 states needs to know which state-specific rule applies to which call - this is a lookup/rules-engine problem, well-scoped for a 30-day build.
- **PII redaction in voice transcripts is an active, unsolved-well problem, not a solved one.** Deepgram ships entity-level redaction; LiveKit shipped PII redaction for its Agent Observability product on 20 Aug 2026 (two weeks before this research); Hamming publishes a dedicated PII-redaction architecture guide. But automated PII detection tops out at 93-95% accuracy and explicitly still needs human review for HIPAA-grade use - meaning the problem isn't closed even by well-funded incumbents, it's an open accuracy gap.
- **No single tool found doing "compliance checker for a specific voice-agent session" as a scoped, protocol-level artifact** (as opposed to platform-wide dashboards bundled into Hamming/Cekura/LiveKit's broader observability suites). The pattern across all three sub-problems (consent-law lookup by state, disclosure-timing check, PII-leakage scan) is the same shape the team already validated works for them: a narrow tool wired directly to AssemblyAI's Voice Agent API session log, not a general observability platform.

**Novelty:** Medium-High. The underlying legal facts (TCPA, state disclosure laws, PII redaction) are well-documented and not novel to research; a standalone tool that ingests one AssemblyAI Voice Agent API session and flags "no AI-disclosure detected in first 15s," "possible SSN/CC-number leak at turn 7," "no consent event logged before this call" as a single scoped report does not appear to exist as a shipped, protocol-specific product - the closest things (Hamming/LiveKit compliance features) are bundled into much bigger paid observability platforms, not a free, narrow, single-purpose checker.

**Completion likelihood (applying the completion report's own method):** High. Every sub-check is a well-known, bounded pattern once implemented: (a) a state-lookup table keyed on area code/dialed number against a fixed rules table - pure data/logic, no tuning loop; (b) a keyword/timing check on the first N seconds of a transcript for disclosure language - mechanical; (c) PII detection via an existing entity-recognition library (spaCy, Presidio, or the AssemblyAI/Deepgram redaction APIs directly) rather than building a detector from scratch - integration, not research. The one real judgment call is picking which jurisdictions/regs to cover for a 30-day demo (scope it to TCPA + California disclosure + basic PII categories, skip HIPAA's full complexity) - a scoping decision, not an open research problem.

**Actual use after 30 days:** High. This is the strongest "would a stranger keep using this" case of any idea researched, for a concrete reason the search evidence supports directly: it targets the *buyer* side of voice AI (a company that has already deployed or is about to deploy a voice agent and needs to not get sued), which is a much larger and more urgent addressable audience than "another QA/dev-tool for the small number of teams building voice agents on AssemblyAI specifically." The compliance-guide publishing frenzy found in this research (six-plus law firms and vendors publishing dedicated 2026 TCPA/AI-disclosure guides in the same few months) is itself evidence of active, current buyer anxiety - exactly the condition under which a free, scoped, open-source compliance checker tends to get real GitHub stars and real forks, the way `garak` did for the adjacent "is my LLM system safe" question. It also fits the partner's domain (voice-AI QA) directly, arguably even more concretely than R2-6, since compliance failures are the kind of finding a QA engineer is specifically tasked with catching before ship.

## Final recommendation

**Don't build R2-5 + R2-7 as the centerpiece.** Across all three axes together, this pairing has the same weak spot twice: both are Low novelty because Hamming AI and Cekura AI already ship near-identical, funded, customer-backed products, and the actual-use research adds a second problem on top - a homegrown copy of a fully-solved commercial category is exactly the shape of project that gets opened for the demo and never again, because the moment you need this tool for real, the commercial version is already better and already there. Fast to build, real problem-fit for the partner, but the team would be spending 30 days re-implementing someone else's shipped SaaS feature, then not using their own copy of it afterward.

**Build R2-6 (Adversarial Voice-Agent Eval Generator) as the centerpiece.** It's the only idea from the original trio that's strong on all three axes at once: Medium-High novelty (the TTS-to-agent-to-judge closed loop for acoustic-cascade failures is real, unaddressed white space, unlike #5/#7), Medium-High completion likelihood (four well-scoped integration points, with the LLM-judge calibration as the one real 30-day risk - manageable, not blocking), and High actual-use likelihood backed by real evidence: `garak` proves the "adversarial eval generator for AI systems" shape sustains years of real open-source use (8,000+ stars, active 2026 releases), and it's the partner's actual professional domain (voice-AI QA red-teaming), not adjacent to it.

**Add the new 21st candidate - a Voice-Agent Compliance/Legal-Risk Checker - as a co-centerpiece or strong second build, not a footnote.** It's the single strongest three-axis score in this entire pass, including against R2-6: real, current regulatory urgency (TCPA penalties, state AI-disclosure fragmentation, an open PII-redaction accuracy gap even incumbents haven't closed) makes actual-use High with direct evidence, not inference; nothing found does this as a scoped, protocol-specific, standalone checker (Medium-High novelty); and every sub-check (state-law lookup, disclosure-timing scan, PII-entity detection via an existing library) is implementation, not research, making it High on completion. It also reframes the hackathon story usefully: instead of "we built more dev tools for people building voice agents" (a crowded lane, per the novelty report), it's "we built the tool for people who just bought one" - a different, less-crowded, and arguably higher-stakes lane, and one the partner's QA background maps onto naturally (catching compliance failures before ship is a QA function).

**If a third leg is wanted, keep R2-7, but scoped and pitched exactly as the novelty report already recommends** - narrowly, as the missing dev tool for AssemblyAI's specific WebSocket protocol, open-source, not "we invented session diffing." Don't add R2-5; its actual-use case is fully subsumed by R2-6's session infrastructure and by the compliance checker's need to read session logs anyway, so building it separately duplicates plumbing without adding a third genuinely distinct axis-of-strength.

**Ideas confirmed dead on all three axes, drop entirely:** R1-2, R1-4, R1-7, R1-12, R2-2 - each has a shipped, funded commercial competitor doing the exact mechanism (Equal Time, Yoodli/LockedIn AI/Huru, Sensi.AI, Gong/Balto/Dialpad, Picovoice Eagle respectively), none has a standing personal use case for either builder, and none is a completion-speed win large enough to offset that.

**Bottom line: swap the centerpiece.** The team's prior leaning (R2-5 + R2-7 centerpiece, R2-6 stretch) inverts under this synthesis. Promote **R2-6** to centerpiece, add the **compliance/legal-risk checker** as a genuine co-centerpiece rather than commentary, and keep R2-7 only as a narrowly-scoped supporting piece if time allows. This isn't a marginal reshuffle - two of the three original picks (R2-5, R2-7) share the identical weakness (a fully-solved commercial category), and the research this report adds independently confirms the novelty report's own reshuffle suggestion while surfacing a genuinely new, better-scoring option the team hadn't considered.
