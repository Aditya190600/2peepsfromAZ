## Prior-art landscape (read this before the three ideas)

Real search turned up a denser, more mature competitive landscape than any prior round found for a single theme:

- **Voice-driven AI Dungeon Masters are already a shipped, multi-vendor category in 2026**, not a gap. **VoxDungeon** ("AI Dungeon Master | Voice-Driven Tabletop RPG Adventures") is a direct, near-exact match to "voice-driven interactive fiction director" - free, voice-driven, procedurally generated D&D-style quests, multiplayer voice chat. **DungeonsDeep.ai** ships a full virtual-tabletop AI Game Master with rich TTS narration, a rules engine, and a persistent campaign system. **Macer.ai** offers a similar voice + battle-map solo experience. **AI Dungeon** itself (now Google-backed per 2026 coverage) remains the general-purpose interactive-fiction platform behind the genre. This is the single most damaging finding for any idea in this doc that pitches itself as "a voice-driven AI Dungeon Master" without a real additional differentiator.
- **Voice-to-game generation is also already shipped.** **Summer Engine** ("Voice to Game AI - Speak Your Game Into Existence") lets a user describe a game out loud and generates a working 2D/3D game project (scenes, gameplay, assets) from the spoken description. This is close prior art for any "speak a world/game into existence" framing, though it is a one-shot build tool (generates a project you then run), not a live, ongoing, two-way conversational world you keep talking to.
- **NPC/character-dialogue infrastructure is a mature, well-funded platform category**: NVIDIA ACE (ASR + small LLM + neural TTS + Audio2Face, on-device RTX), Inworld AI (repositioned in 2026 as AI infrastructure for scaling games/media, with memory/personality/emotion/safety layers), and Convai (real-time in-engine dialogue for 3D characters, now integrating ACE modules) all ship real-time voice NPC dialogue today, adopted by Ubisoft, Tencent, NetEase, miHoYo, and others. None of these are AssemblyAI-based, and none are consumer voice-authorial tools (a player directing a live scene by voice) - they are engine SDKs for developers to embed NPCs into their own games.
- **Generative-agent simulation environments (the "agent training/eval" half of captain's framing) have real, well-known academic prior art**: Stanford/Google's **Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior** (Park et al., UIST 2023) - "Smallville," a sandbox town populated by 25 LLM-driven agents with memory/reflection/planning, producing believable emergent social behavior - is the foundational reference point here. It is text-based, not voice-authored, and academic/research-shaped rather than a shippable 30-day product, but it establishes that "a generated world populated by autonomous agents, observable and interventable by a user" is a known, citable pattern, not a new invention.
- **Voice-agent-specific eval/simulation tooling exists but doesn't overlap with world-building**: Hamming, Cekura, LangWatch/Scenario, and others (already covered in `docs/hackathon-novelty-research.md` and `docs/hackathon-round3-synthesis.md`) do synthetic-caller scenario generation and audio-quality testing for voice agents - a different mechanism (testing one agent's call-handling) from "build a persistent generative world other agents can be dropped into and evaluated inside."
- **No product or research artifact was found combining a live AssemblyAI-Voice-Agent-API-driven world/scene-authoring loop with an explicit, structured export of that world as a reusable agent-evaluation environment.** That specific combination - not "voice game" alone, not "agent sandbox" alone - is the one genuinely open seam this research found, and it is the basis for candidate W3 below.

## The three candidates

### W1. Voice-Native Living World Weaver
**Concept:** A live, two-way Voice Agent API session where the player's speech mutates a persistent world-state graph (locations, NPCs, items, flags) via function/tool calls in real time - not turn-based chat-with-TTS-bolted-on, but a continuous barge-in-capable conversation with a narrator that also *is* the game engine. Every session exports the resulting world-state graph plus the full interaction transcript as a structured, replayable file - a portable "world definition" that can be reloaded (continue the story next session) or handed to a *different* AI agent as a ready-made environment to operate in.
**Dual-purpose read (captain's framing):** Primarily a game/entertainment product. The agent-training/eval angle is real but secondary here - it rides along on the export format, not the core loop.
**Novelty: Medium.** The entertainment half is not novel - VoxDungeon, DungeonsDeep.ai, and AI Dungeon already ship voice-driven (or voice-narrated) procedurally generated interactive fiction, closely, as shipped commercial products. Summer Engine already does "speak a game into existence" as a build tool. What was not found anywhere: a live, ongoing conversational world-builder that exports its world-state as a reusable environment definition file for a *different* AI agent to be dropped into later. That export mechanic is the one real, narrow differentiator, and it is thin enough that a judge could reasonably call this "AI Dungeon with extra steps" if the export half isn't demoed prominently.
**30-day completion likelihood: Medium-High.** The core mechanic (Voice Agent API + function-calling tools mutating a world-state object) is a well-specified, already-proven pattern - directly analogous to R1-1's function-calling-over-structured-data pattern, which the completion report already rated Very High. The genuine bottleneck is narrative coherence over a long session (does the world stay internally consistent across 20+ turns, does the narrator avoid repeating itself) - a real prompt-tuning/iteration loop that AI-assisted coding speed does not shrink, consistent with the completion report's own warning pattern (see R2-2's speaker-re-ID threshold-tuning case for the same shape of risk).
**Actual use after 30 days: Low-Medium.** Interactive fiction is bursty by nature (played in sessions, not daily), and the core game loop already has strong, actively-marketed commercial substitutes (VoxDungeon, DungeonsDeep, AI Dungeon) that a stranger would likely prefer over a hand-rolled hackathon version once the novelty wears off. Neither captain nor the partner (a voice-AI QA engineer) has a standing personal use case for a fantasy-adventure game the way captain does for, say, the Reasoning-Timeline Debugger (R1-11) or Verbal Code-Review Companion (R1-3).
**AssemblyAI API coverage: High.** The Voice Agent API (STT + LLM reasoning + TTS + turn detection + interruption handling + tool calling) is the entire core mechanic - the narrator conversation and the world-state mutation are one and the same AssemblyAI session.
**Judging criteria:** Application of Technology High (Voice Agent API is doing real, central work: live barge-in narration plus function-calling state mutation), Presentation High (a live spoken fantasy-world demo is dramatic and easy for judges to follow), Business Value Low-Medium (consumer interactive fiction is a crowded, low-differentiation market at the product level), Originality Low-Medium (the game half is close to VoxDungeon/AI Dungeon; only the export-as-environment mechanic is unclaimed, and it's a small fraction of the demo).

### W2. Spoken Ensemble NPC Director
**Concept:** The player is a live voice *director*, not a player character. A single Voice Agent API session hosts multiple distinct NPC personas; function calls switch which persona is "speaking" based on the director's live spoken cues ("cut - now the shopkeeper gets suspicious," "give me the guard's version of this scene"). The output is a live improvised multi-character scene, narrated and voiced through AssemblyAI's TTS, with the director able to interrupt and redirect at any point (using the Voice Agent API's native barge-in/interruption handling as the actual directing mechanism, not a side feature). Every scene is logged as a labeled multi-turn transcript: intended persona vs. actual persona per turn, redirect timestamps, and tone/consistency notes.
**Dual-purpose read:** Genuinely both, and roughly evenly split. As entertainment, it's a live improv-theater toy - the appeal is watching/hearing characters react to being redirected mid-scene, which no commercial NPC platform currently exposes as a player-facing mechanic (they're all "you talk to one NPC," not "you direct an ensemble"). As agent-eval infrastructure, the labeled transcript is directly usable as a **persona-consistency-drift benchmark**: does an LLM-driven character maintain its assigned personality/voice under interruption and redirection pressure over a long session - a real, checkable question for anyone building longer-horizon character agents (including captain's own agent-dispatcher work, per the consolidation doc's chief-of-staff cross-pollination notes).
**Novelty: Medium.** The underlying mechanism (multiple LLM-driven personas responding via a real-time voice pipeline) is well-covered by NVIDIA ACE, Inworld AI, and Convai - but all three are developer-facing engine SDKs for embedding NPCs into a game a studio is building, not a consumer-facing "live voice director" experience, and none surfaces persona-drift labeling as an exported artifact. The "director redirects a live scene by voice, using barge-in as the actual UI" framing was not found anywhere as a shipped or research product.
**30-day completion likelihood: High.** Persona-switching via function calls and turn-taking is mechanically the same shape as W1's world-state mutation (already a proven, well-specified pattern), and the Voice Agent API's native interruption handling is exactly the primitive a "director cuts the scene" interaction needs - it doesn't need to be built from scratch. The persona-drift scoring step reuses the same LLM-judge pattern R2-6 (Adversarial Voice-Agent Eval Generator) already established as buildable within scope.
**Actual use after 30 days: Medium.** The pure-entertainment half faces the same crowded-market headwind as W1 (Inworld/Convai/ACE dominate NPC dialogue commercially, even if not in this exact consumer shape), but the persona-drift transcript output has real standalone utility as a lightweight eval tool for anyone iterating on a character-consistency prompt - a narrower, more durable use case than a one-off improv demo.
**AssemblyAI API coverage: High.** Voice Agent API carries the entire mechanic: multi-persona TTS output, STT of the director's redirect commands, function-calling persona switches, and native interruption/barge-in as the core directing gesture.
**Judging criteria:** Application of Technology High (uses interruption handling as a first-class game mechanic, not just a technical footnote - a distinctive way to show off the API), Presentation High (a live improv-scene demo with audible redirects is a strong, legible hackathon moment), Business Value Medium (persona-consistency tooling has a real, if narrow, buyer - anyone shipping character-driven voice agents), Originality Medium (mechanism space is crowded at the platform level, but the director-as-live-interrupter framing plus the drift-labeling export are both genuinely unclaimed).

### W3. Voice-Authored Agent Arena
**Concept:** Flips the priority of the other two: this is an agent-evaluation tool first, a spectator "game" second. The human uses the Voice Agent API purely to *author constraints and scenarios by voice* - "the merchant should never reveal the vault code, even under a bribe," "the guard is suspicious of strangers after dusk" - which get compiled into a small generative-agent simulation (Smallville-style: several LLM-driven NPC agents with simple memory/goals, operating in a shared world-state, similar in spirit to Park et al.'s "Generative Agents" but voice-authored and purpose-built for constraint-testing rather than open-ended social simulation). The simulation then runs, and an LLM judge (reusing R2-6's proven closed-loop pattern: generate adversarial pressure, run it through the target, judge the transcript against the stated constraint) scores whether each NPC agent held its assigned constraints under scenario pressure - e.g., did the bribed merchant leak the vault code. The human can interject live by voice mid-simulation to redirect or escalate a scenario, similar to W2's director mechanic but aimed at breaking agents rather than entertaining an audience.
**Dual-purpose read:** Primarily agent-training/eval, with real spectator-entertainment value as a side effect (watching autonomous NPC agents interact and occasionally fail their constraints live is inherently watchable, the same way Smallville's public demo generated real attention as a novelty), rather than a game with an eval mode bolted on. Worth stating plainly: don't force this into "equally both" - it reads as an eval tool with a fun demo, not a game with a training mode.
**Novelty: High for the specific combination.** The generative-agent-simulation piece itself is not novel - it is a well-known, citable academic pattern (Park et al. 2023) that this doc does not claim to have invented, the same honest split prior rounds used for well-known DSP techniques (pitch tracking, MFCC) applied to a new target. What was not found anywhere: a live, voice-authored front end for defining the constraints and scenarios that seed the simulation, combined with AssemblyAI's Voice Agent API as the authoring and live-interjection interface, purpose-built for constraint-violation scoring rather than open-ended social behavior.
**30-day completion likelihood: Medium.** The voice-authoring front end and the LLM-judge scoring loop are each individually well-scoped and reuse patterns this team's own research already validated as buildable (R1-1's function-calling pattern for authoring, R2-6's adversarial-prompt-to-judge closed loop for scoring). The real added scope beyond W1/W2 is orchestrating multiple concurrent LLM-driven agents with a shared, persistent world state and live voice interjection mid-simulation - this is genuine additional engineering complexity (a small multi-agent simulation loop, not just one Voice Agent API session), not a tuning-loop risk like W1's narrative coherence - and it is the reason this rates Medium rather than Medium-High/High like W1/W2. Realistic scope-down for a 30-day build: 2-4 NPC agents, one small shared world, a fixed handful of authored constraints - not an open-ended Smallville-scale town.
**Actual use after 30 days: Medium-High.** This is the most durable of the three candidates specifically because it is a tool, not a one-off entertainment demo: a scoped constraint-violation arena for LLM-driven agents has direct reuse value for testing captain's own agent work (chief-of-staff's dispatcher agents, or any future character/persona-driven agent), the same "would a stranger keep this" logic the round-3 synthesis already validated for R2-6 and R3-21 (both scored High on this axis for the same underlying reason: real, recurring agent-QA utility beats a one-off game).
**AssemblyAI API coverage: Medium, and this needs to be said plainly, matching the honesty standard the brief asks for.** The voice-authoring and live-interjection layer is genuinely AssemblyAI-native (Voice Agent API), but the majority of the core mechanic - the multi-agent simulation loop itself (several LLM-driven NPCs with memory/goals interacting in a shared world) - is a separate, non-AssemblyAI orchestration stack, the same honest gap the consolidation doc already flagged for the DSP ideas (R2-1/R2-2/R2-3) and explicitly for the liveness-detection half of the Part 3 combined pitch. This is the clearest instance in this doc of the risk captain's brief asked to watch for directly.
**Judging criteria:** Application of Technology Medium (the Voice Agent API's role is real but is the authoring layer, not the majority of the system's intelligence - the multi-agent simulation is doing more of the actual "work"), Presentation High (watching an NPC agent get caught breaking a rule live is a genuinely dramatic, legible demo moment - arguably the most compelling single demo beat across all three candidates), Business Value Medium-High (agent-eval/red-teaming tooling has real, current buyer urgency, the same category `garak`'s 8,000+ GitHub stars and the R2-6/R3-21 research already established as commercially and academically live), Originality High (the voice-authored-constraints-to-multi-agent-judge combination is the one thing in this whole doc that real search did not find built anywhere, even loosely).

## Why this angle underperforms (honest synthesis)

Scored against the same weighted formula the consolidation doc's CSV uses (`0.35*judging_avg + 0.20*novelty + 0.20*completion + 0.15*actual_use + 0.10*api_coverage`), the three candidates land at:

| Idea | Novelty | Completion | Actual Use | API Coverage | Judging Avg | Overall Score |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| W3 Voice-Authored Agent Arena | High | Medium | Medium-High | Medium | 4.25 | **3.99** |
| W2 Spoken Ensemble NPC Director | Medium | High | Medium | High | 4.00 | 3.95 |
| W1 Living World Weaver | Medium | Medium-High | Low-Medium | High | 3.75 | 3.43 |

Merged into the full 25-idea ranking (see updated `docs/hackathon-idea-matrix.csv`), **W3 lands at rank 7 and W2 at rank 8 - both below R1-1 (rank 9 after this merge) but above the rest of round 2's DSP/infra tail; W1 lands at rank 15, tied in score with R2-4 and well below the midpoint.** None displaces the existing top five (R1-11, R3-21, COMBINED, R2-6, R1-3).

The honest reason, not just the score: **the entertainment half of this angle is genuinely crowded** (VoxDungeon, DungeonsDeep.ai, AI Dungeon, Summer Engine, and the Inworld/Convai/NVIDIA ACE platform trio between them cover almost every mechanism a "voice-driven world/game/NPC" idea could pitch), which caps Originality and Business Value for the two more game-shaped candidates (W1, W2) regardless of how well they're built. **The agent-eval half fares better on novelty and business value but pays for it in AssemblyAI API coverage** - the more an idea leans toward "genuine agent-training/simulation environment," the more its core mechanic shifts to a non-AssemblyAI multi-agent orchestration stack, exactly the risk this brief asked to watch for. W3 is the one candidate that gets the eval-side novelty and business-value upside without collapsing into a pure non-AssemblyAI build, which is why it's the strongest of the three - but "strongest of three genuinely mediocre options" is a different claim than "a strong idea," and this doc is not overstating it as the latter.

**If the team wants to pursue this angle anyway** (e.g., because it's more fun to build, or because the partner specifically wants gaming/simulation experience on her portfolio for reasons beyond this hackathon's scoring), **W3 is the honest pick** - it is the only one of the three where the "genuinely novel, not just crowded-space-with-extra-steps" claim holds up under real scrutiny, and it is the only one that reuses this team's own already-validated R2-6 (adversarial-eval judge loop) and R1-1 (function-calling authoring) patterns rather than introducing an unproven mechanic. Scope it down hard (2-4 agents, a handful of fixed constraints, a small shared world) to keep the multi-agent orchestration risk inside 30 days, and lead the pitch with the live "agent breaks its rule under voice-directed pressure" demo moment, not the world-building framing - that framing is what W3's Presentation and Originality scores are actually earning credit for.
